# HCOS™ Organizational Readiness Core Concepts

**Document Type:** Architecture and Terminology  
**Status:** Draft  
**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Date:** July 31, 2026  
**Related ADR:** ADR-001 — Organizational Readiness Stewardship

## Purpose

This document distinguishes the core constructs used in the HCOS™ Organizational Readiness architecture. These terms should not be used interchangeably.

## Organizational Readiness

> **The overall capacity of a human system to understand, absorb, govern, sustain, and learn from a significant change.**

It includes system understanding, purpose, evidence, authority, governance, resources, workflow capacity, human capacity, communication, measurement, resilience, and learning.

Readiness is contextual. An organization may be ready for one use case and unready for another.

## System Readiness

> **The degree to which workflows, policies, information pathways, roles, resources, metrics, and operating conditions can support a proposed change without creating unmanaged dysfunction or harm.**

System readiness does not require perfection. It requires important dependencies, weaknesses, and compensatory work to be visible enough for responsible decision-making.

## Decision Readiness

> **The degree to which accountable leaders and affected participants possess sufficient system understanding, evidence, governance, and authority to make a responsible decision about whether and how to proceed.**

Decision readiness does not mean certainty. It means uncertainty is sufficiently understood, bounded, governed, and disclosed.

## Decision Uncertainty

> **The unresolved uncertainty surrounding the anticipated value, consequences, responsibilities, risks, human effects, and reversibility of a proposed change.**

It may arise from incomplete evidence, unclear workflows, ambiguous authority, undefined accountability, unpredictable costs, immature governance, unknown failure modes, and unclear exception burden.

Uncertainty becomes dangerous when it is hidden, minimized, transferred to others, or treated as resolved without sufficient evidence.

## Implementation Readiness

> **The practical capacity to execute an approved change under defined conditions.**

It includes implementation ownership, configuration, integration, training, communication, staffing, support, escalation, testing, deployment sequencing, and rollback capacity.

An organization may be implementation-ready but not decision-ready.

## Adoption Readiness

> **The degree to which intended users and affected participants can understand, meaningfully engage with, and appropriately use or respond to the change.**

It includes role clarity, perceived usefulness, trust, training, workflow fit, professional judgment, psychological safety, communication, and participation.

Adoption readiness should not be reduced to compliance or willingness.

## Governance Readiness

> **The degree to which purpose, authority, accountability, boundaries, oversight, escalation, review, and correction are defined before implementation.**

## Evidence Readiness

> **The degree to which a decision is supported by relevant, trustworthy, sufficiently complete, and appropriately interpreted information.**

Evidence readiness requires a current-state baseline, problem evidence, benefit and risk evidence, provenance, limitations, uncertainty, and attention to conflicting findings.

## Human Readiness

> **The degree to which affected people possess the capacity, understanding, support, agency, and protections needed to participate without preventable harm or unreasonable compensatory burden.**

This is not a judgment of individual resilience. It is an assessment of whether the system creates appropriate conditions for people.

## Learning Readiness

> **The capacity to detect outcomes, identify unintended consequences, receive feedback, revise assumptions, correct failures, and adapt the intervention over time.**

## Resilience and Change Capacity

> **The ability to absorb disruption, maintain essential functions, recover from failure, and revise the intervention without transferring unmanaged pressure to people.**

Resilience should not mean expecting employees to endure poor design.

## Relationship Among the Constructs

```text
System Understanding
        ↓
Evidence Readiness
        ↓
Decision Readiness
        ↓
Governance Decision
        ↓
Implementation Readiness
        ↓
Adoption and Human Readiness
        ↓
Responsible Implementation
        ↓
Learning Readiness
        ↓
Adaptation and Stewardship
```

Organizational readiness encompasses the full sequence.

## Unit of Assessment

The preferred unit is:

> **The organization’s readiness to introduce a defined AI capability into a defined workflow for a defined purpose, affecting defined people, under defined governance conditions.**

## Readiness Is Not Certification

The assessment will not certify that an organization is universally safe, compliant, ethical, technically secure, ready for all AI uses, or guaranteed to achieve value.

It will identify strengths, uncertainty, gaps, decision-critical conditions, required protections, evidence needs, and appropriate next steps.

## Readiness Is Not a Single Score

A high aggregate score must not override critical gaps such as:

- no accountable owner;
- no clear problem definition;
- no escalation path;
- no baseline;
- unresolved high-consequence risk;
- inability to identify affected people;
- automation of an incoherent workflow;
- reliance on invisible human compensation.

## HCOS 8 + 3 + 1 Integration

The constructs should be evaluated through:

- the eight system forces;
- the three human protections;
- the one meaning anchor.

## Core Terminology Principle

> Readiness does not mean the absence of uncertainty. It means the organization understands enough to govern uncertainty responsibly, protect people, and learn before consequences become invisible or irreversible.
